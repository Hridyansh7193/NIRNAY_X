Abstract—Clinicians rarely diagnose Alzheimer’s disease (AD)
from a single source. They read a structural brain MRI alongside
cognitive test scores, demographic details, and clinical biomark-
ers. Most deep learning models for AD don’t work this way. They
use only one of these inputs, or they assume every patient’s
data can be gathered in one place, which hospitals are often
not allowed to do. They also say little about why they made a
given prediction. We present FEDXAI, a federated multimodal
framework for AD diagnosis that keeps patient data on site and
explains its predictions. A 3D Convolutional Neural Network (3D
CNN) encodes the MRI volume, a multilayer perceptron (MLP)
encodes the clinical variables, and intermediate fusion combines
the two feature vectors. Each institution trains on its own data, so
raw scans and records never leave the premises. Only model up-
dates are shared, clipped and perturbed with Gaussian noise once
per round, then combined with Federated Averaging (FedAvg).
For each prediction, 3D Grad-CAM highlights the brain regions
involved and SHAP ranks the clinical variables. We describe
the framework’s design and a protocol for evaluating it on data
from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)
under centralized, IID, and non-IID training. The software is
implemented and has run end to end on ADNI clinical data with
synthetic placeholder images. We haven’t used real MRI scans
yet, so we report no accuracy results.
Index Terms—Federated learning, explainable AI, multimodal
deep learning, 3D CNN, privacy, medical diagnosis, Grad-CAM,
SHAP, non-IID data, FedAvg, Alzheimer’s disease.
I. INTRODUCTION
Deep learning already does well on many medical imaging
tasks, and on some radiology and pathology problems its ac-
curacy comes close to that of specialists. Getting these models
into everyday clinical practice is harder. A doctor almost never
decides from a scan alone. The scan is read together with the

patient’s history, examination findings, cognitive test results,
and risk factors such as age [1]. A model that sees only one
of these sources misses useful context, and that can make it
less sensitive and less robust [2].
Two more problems make multimodal models hard to build
across hospitals. The first is privacy. Laws such as HIPAA in
the United States and GDPR in the European Union strictly
limit how health records and medical images can be shared,
and copying patient data into a central database carries legal
and institutional risk [3]. So each hospital often ends up
training on its own small cohort, and models trained that way
tend to overfit and generalize poorly. The second is trust. A
deep network doesn’t explain its output, and clinicians can’t
reasonably act on a probability score when they have no way
to check the reasoning behind it [4].
Federated Learning (FL) sidesteps the data-sharing problem.
Several institutions train one model together without moving
their data [5]. Each site keeps its records locally and sends
only model parameters (or gradients) to a coordinating server
over an encrypted connection, which in our case is gRPC
secured with TLS. In FEDXAI, the server combines these
updates with Federated Averaging (FedAvg) [5], weighting
each client’s parameters by the number of samples it holds.
FedAvg means data no longer has to be centralized, but the
differences between sites remain. Patient populations, clinical
protocols, and scanners vary from one hospital to the next, so
each client’s data is not independent and identically distributed
(non-IID) [6].
Explainable AI (XAI) methods can show what a trained
model relies on. Grad-CAM [7] produces heatmaps over the
input image, and SHAP [8] assigns an importance value to
each input feature. But most earlier work treated FL and
XAI separately, or applied XAI only to image-only models.
Much less is known about whether the explanations from
a multimodal model stay reliable when it’s trained across
heterogeneous federated clients.

II. RELATEDWORK
Our work draws on four lines of research.
A. Deep learning for Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis
CNNs are a common choice for classifying Alzheimer’s
disease from structural MRI [9], [10]. Qiuet al.[9] combined
MRI with routinely collected clinical data to separate normal
cognition, mild cognitive impairment, and dementia, and used
SHAP to tie the model’s decisions to brain regions. A model
that sees only the image can’t use what’s in clinical notes and
test scores [11], which is why FEDXAI pairs its 3D CNN with
a clinical branch.
B. Multimodal fusion for clinical diagnosis
Fusion methods are usually grouped into early fusion of
inputs, late fusion of predictions, and intermediate fusion
of learned features [2], [11]. Models that combine imaging
with electronic health record data have generally outperformed
single-modality models [2], [11], since the two sources carry
complementary information [1], [11]. But most are trained on
data pooled at one site, which is hard to arrange under current
privacy rules [1], [3].
C. Federated learning and privacy protection
FL lets institutions train a model without exchanging patient
records [3], [10], [12]. In FedAvg [5], the server averages
the client updates, weighted by dataset size. Applications
include ADMarker [12], which detects digital biomarkers of
Alzheimer’s disease from sensor data recorded in natural living
environments, and FedVC-ADDiM [10], which diagnoses the
disease from MRI scans held at different sites. There is also
a benchmark of FL algorithms for medical image classifica-
tion [13]. Shared parameters can still reveal information about
the training data, so differential privacy is often added [3],
[14]. Two problems remain. Non-IID client data slows conver-
gence and can lower accuracy [6], [13], and federated medical
studies say little about whether the aggregated model is still
interpretable [13]. Our protocol therefore evaluates FedAvg
under controlled Dirichlet label skew [15].
D. Explainable AI
Clinicians are more likely to adopt a model whose decisions
they can inspect [4]. Grad-CAM [7] builds a heatmap from
the gradients flowing into the last convolutional layer. For a
3D layer, the result is a volume that can be overlaid on the
scan. SHAP [8] uses Shapley values to attribute a prediction
to tabular features. Jahanet al.[16] used SHAP to explain
a federated multimodal AD model. But explanation quality
can’t be taken for granted. Jinet al.[17] tested 16 heatmap
methods on a multimodal medical imaging task, and none
reliably reflected the model’s decision process.
E. Research gap and our approach
Four things are missing from the work above. Image-only
3D CNNs ignore clinical information [9]. Fusion models
usually assume centralized data [2], [11]. Federated studies
concentrate on accuracy [6], [13]. And federated XAI studies
have rarely paired a visual explanation with a tabular one for
a single fused model [16], [17]. FEDXAI is built to address
all four. It fuses a 3D CNN and an MLP (Sections III-C
and III-D), trains the fused model with FedAvg and clipped,
noise-perturbed updates (Section III-E), and explains each
prediction for both modalities (Section III-F).

be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive
impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer’s disease (AD).
The clinical variables come from the ADNI study ta-
bles [18]. They are demographics (age, sex, education), cog-
nitive scores (MMSE, CDR-SB, ADAS-Cog13), hippocampal

V. IMPLEMENTATION STATUS AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL
A. Implementation status
All components of Section III are implemented: the two
encoders and the fusion classifier, FedAvg training over Flower
with clipped and noise-perturbed client updates, and the Grad-
CAM and SHAP explanation modules. The pipeline has run
end to end on the real ADNI clinical variables, with synthetic
volumes standing in for MRI scans. These runs confirm that
the software executes, but we don’t report their accuracy for
two reasons. First, each synthetic volume was random noise
whose intensity depended on the diagnosis label, so the image
revealed the label, making any result involving the imaging
branch meaningless. Second, the federated runs were scored
on subjects that had also been used for training. The protocol
below fixes both problems.

B. Planned comparisons
We will make four comparisons on real ADNI scans.
(1)Fusion:under centralized training, we compare the fused
model with an MRI-only 3D CNN and a clinical-only MLP.
(2)Federation:we train the fused model with FedAvg on
IID clients and on Dirichlet splits withα∈{1.0,0.5,0.1},
and compare the results with centralized training. (3)Update
protection:we run each federated setting with and without
clipping and noise. (4)Label leakage:we also train every
model with a clinical branch, excluding MMSE and CDR-SB.
C. Measures
We will score all models only on the held-out test subjects,
which no client sees during training. The classes are unbal-
anced, so we will report accuracy, macro-averaged F1, one-
vs-rest AUC, and per-class sensitivity, as mean and standard
deviation over three random seeds. To compare convergence,
we will record test accuracy at each communication round. For
the explanations, we will compare the Grad-CAM maps with
the brain regions known to be affected in AD, and compare
the SHAP rankings of the clinical features across clients.

VI. CONCLUSION
We presented the design of FEDXAI, a federated multi-
modal framework for Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis that keeps
patient records at the hospital and explains its predictions. A
3D CNN and an MLP encoder are fused at the feature level
and trained with FedAvg. Each client’s update is clipped and
perturbed with Gaussian noise, and every prediction comes
with a 3D Grad-CAM heatmap and SHAP attributions. The
software is implemented, and we have defined a protocol for
evaluating it.
The work has real limitations. We have no accuracy results
yet, because the framework hasn’t been trained on real MRI
scans. We claim no formal privacy guarantee: clipping is
applied to each client’s whole update, the noise level is low,
and we do no privacy accounting. MMSE and CDR-SB are
part of the ADNI diagnosis, so their contribution has to be
measured by ablation. The32 3 input limits Grad-CAM to
coarse regions. And the clients will be simulated by splitting
a single dataset.
Next we will run the evaluation on real ADNI scans.

accounting and secure aggregation, and to test on real multi-
